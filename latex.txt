\section{Abstract}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks (VANETs) form the communication backbone of modern
intelligent transportation systems, enabling vehicles to exchange safety-critical data
over Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) links in real time.
The open wireless medium that enables this cooperation simultaneously exposes the
network to cyberattacks including Denial-of-Service flooding, Sybil identity fabrication,
false data injection, and radio-frequency jamming.

This project implements and evaluates a multi-model intrusion detection system for
VANETs using the VeReMi (Vehicular Reference Misbehaviour) dataset. Three model
families are studied: classical ensemble classifiers (Random Forest and XGBoost), a
deep learning architecture (ConvGRU combining convolutional and gated recurrent units),
and an adversarial evaluation pipeline based on the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM).
Time-windowed feature engineering transforms raw beacon traces into compact statistical
feature vectors that capture the physical-layer signatures — position consistency,
speed magnitude, acceleration variance, and heading-speed alignment — that are
theoretically grounded in VANET wireless metrics.

Experimental results on a balanced 3\,104-sample subset drawn from 22 million records
demonstrate that Random Forest achieves 91.7\% accuracy and XGBoost 93.9\% under
clean conditions. Under FGSM adversarial perturbation, Random Forest collapses to
50.0\%, validating the critical vulnerability identified in the literature. Adversarial
retraining restores performance to 94.6\% for Random Forest, exceeding its original
baseline. The ConvGRU model achieves 69.5\% accuracy, consistent with the inherent
challenge of learning temporal patterns from small sequential windows with limited data.
These results comprehensively validate the literature's core claims and expose the
adversarial robustness gap as the most pressing open problem in VANET IDS research.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} VANETs, Intrusion Detection, Random Forest, XGBoost, ConvGRU,
Adversarial Robustness, FGSM, VeReMi Dataset, Wireless Security

\section{Introduction}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Connected vehicles have gradually transformed public roads into distributed wireless
sensor networks. Each vehicle simultaneously generates, relays, and consumes data.
Safety-critical applications — collision avoidance, emergency pre-emption, road hazard
broadcasts — tolerate no ambiguity: a corrupted or delayed message can have immediate
physical consequences.

Detecting intrusions in this environment is qualitatively harder than classical network
security for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \textbf{Continuous topology change:} Vehicle mobility reshapes the network graph
    every few seconds, making static rule sets obsolete.
    \item \textbf{Physical-layer attacks:} Jamming and RSSI spoofing operate below the
    layer where packet contents are visible, so payload inspection alone arrives too late.
    \item \textbf{Hardware constraints:} On-Board Units (OBUs) have tightly limited CPU,
    memory, and power budgets — models comfortable on a server may be entirely impractical
    for embedded deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Project Objectives}

This project aims to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item Implement a complete IDS pipeline on the VeReMi VANET dataset, from raw
    trace data through time-window feature engineering to classification.
    \item Compare classical ML (Random Forest, XGBoost) against a deep learning
    approach (ConvGRU) using a consistent evaluation protocol.
    \item Quantitatively validate the adversarial vulnerability gap identified in the
    literature survey, and demonstrate that adversarial retraining mitigates it.
    \item Connect experimental outcomes to specific claims in the surveyed literature,
    providing an empirical grounding for the theoretical arguments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Document Organisation}

Section~3 provides the background on VANETs and wireless attack types. Section~4
surveys five representative works from the literature. Section~5 describes the VeReMi
dataset. Section~6 details the feature engineering methodology. Sections~7--9 present
the three model families and their implementation. Section~10 covers adversarial attacks
and defences. Section~11 analyses results comprehensively. Section~12 discusses findings
in relation to the literature, and Section~13 concludes with future directions.

\section{Background}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{VANET Architecture}

A VANET is a mobile ad hoc network whose nodes are road vehicles. Each vehicle carries
an OBU that periodically broadcasts Basic Safety Messages (BSMs) over the IEEE\,802.11p
/ DSRC standard, advertising position, speed, heading, and local hazard information.
Fixed Roadside Units (RSUs) supplement the vehicle-to-vehicle fabric with infrastructure
anchors.

Three physical-layer metrics carry particularly rich information about channel condition:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item[RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator):] A power-level measurement at the
    receiver. A sender claiming to be 500\,m away but whose signal arrives at the strength
    expected from 50\,m presents a physical inconsistency that is very difficult to fake
    simultaneously with crafting convincing message content.
    \item[PDR (Packet Delivery Ratio):] The fraction of transmitted packets that arrive
    successfully. Channel flooding degrades PDR through induced collisions and buffer
    saturation.
    \item[PSR (Packet Send Ratio):] The rate at which a node actively transmits. Spikes
    in PSR at a single node, while PDR collapses network-wide, are a reliable DoS
    signature.
\end{description}

\subsection{Taxonomy of VANET Attacks}

The attacks addressed across the surveyed and implemented work fall into three broad
families:

\subsubsection{Availability Attacks}
Denial-of-Service flooding saturates channel capacity or exhausts OBU processing.
Distributed DoS coordinates multiple compromised nodes to amplify this effect. Jamming
injects broadband noise that elevates the noise floor and forces receivers to discard every
frame.

\subsubsection{Authenticity Attacks}
Sybil attacks manufacture multiple fictitious vehicle identities from a single physical
transmitter. False message injection inserts well-formed but fabricated events (phantom
accidents, ghost queues) into the cooperative awareness layer. Position falsification
corrupts GPS coordinates in beacons while keeping all other fields plausible.

\subsubsection{Confidentiality Attacks}
These expose driver identity or vehicle trajectory, often as a precursor to targeted
surveillance. They are less relevant to the current IDS scope but inform the privacy
architecture of the cryptographic layer.

\section{Literature Survey}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Class-Aware Oversampling for Robust Intrusion Detection in VANETs}
\textit{Discover Computing, Springer, 2024~\cite{kapoor2024}}

\textbf{Problem and Approach.}
Attack events are statistically rare compared to normal communication. A model that
labels every sample as benign achieves high aggregate accuracy while detecting nothing
useful. Naive global oversampling introduces its own hazard: synthetic samples near
the majority class inject noise precisely where the decision boundary is most sensitive.
This paper reframes oversampling as a geometry problem: the feature space is projected
to two dimensions using t-SNE, making spatial relationships between class clusters
visible and computationally addressable. Oversampling is applied selectively — minority
attack classes whose clusters sit close to the majority boundary receive aggressive
augmentation via SMOTE and ADASYN, while well-separated clusters are largely left
alone. A convolutional classifier is then trained on the augmented CICIDS2017 data.

\textbf{Results and Limitations.}
Geometry-aware oversampling consistently outperforms both unaugmented and uniformly
oversampled baselines across precision, recall, and F1-score for low-frequency attack
categories. However, CICIDS2017 captures enterprise-style traffic rather than V2V
wireless exchanges, and the framework does not use RSSI or PDR as features. The
t-SNE preprocessing step adds meaningful computational overhead.

\subsection{ML-Based Cryptographic Protocols for Intrusion Detection (ML-CPIDS)}
\textit{Scientific Reports, Nature Portfolio, 2023~\cite{liu2023}}

\textbf{Problem and Approach.}
Static detectors assume a reasonably stable environment. VANETs violate every such
assumption — nodes appear and vanish continuously, and insider adversaries already hold
valid credentials. ML-CPIDS builds a two-layer defence: pseudonymous certificate-based
authentication at the communication layer, and an adaptive ML layer above it that
monitors communication metadata for behavioural anomalies.

\textbf{Results and Limitations.}
ML-CPIDS achieves lower end-to-end latency than comparable purely cryptographic
designs while delivering stronger detection against insider attacks. The key contribution
is demonstrating that authentication and intrusion detection are complementary: a node
that is authenticated is not necessarily trustworthy. The ML layer operates on
higher-layer metadata, so sub-packet-level attacks such as RSSI manipulation and
jamming remain outside its field of view.

\subsection{Cascaded Deep Learning for DoS Detection with Multi-Variant Metaheuristic}
\textit{Sensors, MDPI, 2023~\cite{manderna2023}}

\textbf{Problem and Approach.}
Effective DoS detection requires models that understand both the local packet-level
signature of an attack burst and the longer temporal arc of a campaign. The
proposed architecture chains a Cascaded CNN (CCNN) with a Self-Attention
Bidirectional LSTM (SA-BiLSTM). The CCNN extracts spatial correlations from
individual feature vectors; the SA-BiLSTM models traffic dynamics in both temporal
directions and weights the most discriminative time steps through attention. A
Multi-Variant Gradient-Based Optimisation (MV-GBO) metaheuristic automatically
tunes hyperparameters of both components.

\textbf{Results and Limitations.}
The CCNN + SA-BiLSTM system outperforms single-component baselines across multiple
DoS sub-types. However, two distinct deep networks in cascade plus a metaheuristic
optimiser create a computational pipeline too heavy for OBU-grade embedded hardware.

\subsection{RSSI Sequence and Vehicle Driving Matrix Based Sybil Detection}
\textit{IEEE ICCSN, 2019~\cite{li2019}}

\textbf{Problem and Approach.}
A Sybil attacker presents multiple fake identities from a single physical transmitter.
RSDM (RSSI Sequence and Driving Matrix) grounds detection in physics: each beacon
carries claimed position and velocity. The detector collects the received RSSI sequence
and compares it against the sequence that should result from a transmitter following the
kinematics encoded in the beacon, given the free-space propagation model. A single
physical transmitter cannot be in different spatial positions simultaneously — its RSSI
readings can match the expected trajectory for at most one claimed identity.

\textbf{Results and Limitations.}
RSDM achieves high Sybil detection rates with low false-positives. Detection is fully
distributed and requires no server round-trips. However, a sophisticated adversary
who can simultaneously spoof GNSS signals can forge a kinematically consistent RSSI
sequence. RSDM also does not generalise beyond Sybil detection.

\subsection{AI-Driven Ensemble Classifier for Jamming Attack Detection}
\textit{IEEE Access, 2023~\cite{elshafai2023}}

\textbf{Problem and Approach.}
Jamming can render every nearby vehicle's communications unintelligible at negligible
hardware cost, and packet-content analysis is useless once a jam is in progress.
Fourteen classical ML algorithms and four deep learning architectures are evaluated
on channel-level features (busy time, noise power, collision rate). The three strongest
performers — Random Forest, Extra Tree, and CNN — are combined into a majority-vote
ensemble.

\textbf{Results and Limitations.}
The RF + ET + CNN ensemble achieves 99.81\% accuracy, confirming that model
diversity provides meaningful gains over any individual strong learner. Running three
models at inference time multiplies computational cost, and the evaluation scope is
limited exclusively to jamming.

\section{Feature Engineering: Time-Window Approach}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Motivation}

Raw per-beacon records are point-in-time snapshots. Individual beacons are ambiguous:
a single high-speed reading is not necessarily an attack, but a sustained pattern of
anomalous speed, inconsistent positions, and heading–speed misalignment over a one-second
window is highly diagnostic. This section details the time-windowing approach that
converts raw traces into structured feature vectors suitable for classification.

\subsection{Window Construction}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Time-window feature construction for one attack scenario.},
                   label=lst:windows]
WINDOW_SIZE = 1.0  # seconds

for at_name, at_group in df.groupby('attack_type'):
    at_sorted = at_group.sort_values('rcvTime').reset_index(drop=True)
    t_min = at_sorted['rcvTime'].min()
    at_sorted['time_bin'] = (
        (at_sorted['rcvTime'] - t_min) / WINDOW_SIZE
    ).astype(int)

    for tb, win in at_sorted.groupby('time_bin'):
        if len(win) < 3:   # skip degenerate windows
            continue
        # label: majority attack if >30% of messages are labelled 1
        attack_rate = win['attack'].mean()
        label = 1 if attack_rate > 0.3 else 0
\end{lstlisting}

A window is labelled as an attack if more than 30\% of its constituent beacons carry an
attack label. This threshold was chosen to be robust to sparse but persistent
misbehaviour while avoiding false positives from momentarily anomalous but legitimate
messages.

\subsection{Derived Feature Groups}

\subsubsection{Speed Features}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Speed magnitude and statistics.}, label=lst:speed_feats]
spd_mag = np.sqrt(win['spd_0']**2 + win['spd_1']**2)

feat.update({
    'spd_x_mean': win['spd_0'].mean(),
    'spd_x_std':  win['spd_0'].std(),
    'spd_y_mean': win['spd_1'].mean(),
    'spd_y_std':  win['spd_1'].std(),
    'spd_mag_mean': spd_mag.mean(),
    'spd_mag_std':  spd_mag.std(),
    'spd_mag_max':  spd_mag.max(),
})
\end{lstlisting}

Speed magnitude directly reflects the \texttt{RandomSpeed}, \texttt{ConstSpeed}, and
\texttt{RandomSpeedOffset} attack families, where an attacker injects unrealistic velocity
values.

\subsubsection{Heading--Speed Consistency (Cosine Similarity)}

A physically grounded consistency check compares the reported heading direction against
the reported velocity vector:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Heading--speed cosine similarity.}, label=lst:hs_cos]
hed_mag = np.sqrt(win['hed_0']**2 + win['hed_1']**2)
hs_dot  = win['hed_0'] * win['spd_0'] + win['hed_1'] * win['spd_1']
hs_cos  = (hs_dot / (hed_mag * spd_mag + 1e-8)).clip(-1, 1)

feat.update({
    'hs_cos_mean': hs_cos.mean(),
    'hs_cos_std':  hs_cos.std(),
})
\end{lstlisting}

A vehicle whose heading vector is perpendicular to its velocity vector is physically
impossible under normal driving conditions. Attackers who fabricate speed values without
correspondingly adjusting heading produce a characteristically low cosine similarity.

\subsubsection{Position and Acceleration Features}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Position and acceleration feature extraction.}, label=lst:pos_acl]
acl_mag       = np.sqrt(win['acl_0']**2 + win['acl_1']**2)
pos_noise_mag = np.sqrt(win['pos_noise_0']**2 + win['pos_noise_1']**2)
spd_noise_mag = np.sqrt(win['spd_noise_0']**2 + win['spd_noise_1']**2)

feat.update({
    'pos_range_x':    win['pos_0'].max() - win['pos_0'].min(),
    'pos_range_y':    win['pos_1'].max() - win['pos_1'].min(),
    'acl_mag_mean':   acl_mag.mean(),
    'acl_mag_std':    acl_mag.std(),
    'pos_noise_mean': pos_noise_mag.mean(),
    'pos_noise_std':  pos_noise_mag.std(),
    'spd_noise_mean': spd_noise_mag.mean(),
    'spd_noise_std':  spd_noise_mag.std(),
})
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Message Count}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Message count as a DoS indicator.}, label=lst:msg_count]
feat['msg_count'] = len(win)
\end{lstlisting}

Message count per unit time is the primary indicator of DoS attacks. A normal vehicle
transmits BSMs at approximately 10\,Hz; a DoS node may transmit at several hundred
messages per second within the same window.

\subsection{Sampling and Balancing}

The full feature-engineered dataset contains thousands of windows per attack scenario.
To ensure tractable training and class balance, a stratified 1\,552-sample subset per
class (3\,104 total) is drawn, preserving the proportion of attack scenarios within the
positive class:

\section{Deep Learning: ConvGRU Architecture}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Motivation}

Classical classifiers treat each feature vector as an independent sample. They cannot
naturally capture how VANET metrics evolve across a \textit{sequence} of windows, nor
adapt internal representations as traffic patterns shift. The ConvGRU architecture
addresses both shortcomings while remaining lightweight enough for embedded OBU
deployment, as discussed in the literature survey~\cite{suman2025}.

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={ConvGRU model definition in Keras.}, label=lst:convgru]
import tensorflow as tf
from tensorflow.keras import layers, models

def build_convgru(input_shape, num_classes=1):
    inputs = tf.keras.Input(shape=input_shape)

    # CNN branch: spatial feature extraction
    x = layers.Conv1D(64, kernel_size=3, activation='relu',
                      padding='same')(inputs)
    x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
    x = layers.Conv1D(128, kernel_size=3, activation='relu',
                      padding='same')(x)
    x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
    x = layers.MaxPooling1D(pool_size=2)(x)
    x = layers.Dropout(0.3)(x)

    # GRU branch: temporal sequence modelling
    x = layers.GRU(64, return_sequences=False)(x)
    x = layers.Dropout(0.3)(x)

    # Classification head
    x = layers.Dense(64, activation='relu')(x)
    x = layers.Dropout(0.2)(x)
    outputs = layers.Dense(1, activation='sigmoid')(x)

    model = models.Model(inputs, outputs)
    model.compile(
        optimizer=tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.001),
        loss='binary_crossentropy',
        metrics=['accuracy']
    )
    return model
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Design Rationale}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item[Conv1D layers:] Two convolutional layers with 64 and 128 filters and kernel
    size 3 extract local structural patterns across adjacent feature dimensions. Batch
    normalisation stabilises training by reducing internal covariate shift. This mirrors
    the CNN branch of the ConvGRU described in the literature~\cite{suman2025}.
    \item[MaxPooling1D:] Reduces sequence length by half, providing translation
    invariance and reducing parameter count for the subsequent GRU layer.
    \item[GRU layer:] A single GRU with 64 units processes the pooled CNN output as a
    sequence, capturing how the extracted spatial features evolve over time. GRUs are
    preferred over LSTMs because they match sequential modelling performance with fewer
    parameters and lower inference latency, which is critical for OBU deployment.
    \item[Dropout (0.3 / 0.2):] Applied after convolutional blocks and GRU to prevent
    co-adaptation of neurons under the relatively small training set.
\end{description}

\subsection{Sequence Preparation}

Since the ConvGRU expects 3-D input (samples, timesteps, features), the 2-D feature
matrix is reshaped:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Reshaping features for ConvGRU input.}, label=lst:reshape]
SEQ_LEN = 4   # 4 consecutive windows as one sequence

# Reshape X_train: (n_samples, SEQ_LEN, n_features)
X_train_seq = X_train[:len(X_train) - (len(X_train) % SEQ_LEN)]
X_train_seq = X_train_seq.reshape(-1, SEQ_LEN,
                                   X_train.shape[1])
y_train_seq = y_train[:len(X_train_seq) * SEQ_LEN:SEQ_LEN]
\end{lstlisting}

Each sequence consists of 4 consecutive 1-second windows, giving the GRU a 4-second
temporal context per inference step.

\subsection{Training}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={ConvGRU training loop.}, label=lst:convgru_train]
model = build_convgru((SEQ_LEN, X_train.shape[1]))

history = model.fit(
    X_train_seq, y_train_seq,
    epochs=50,
    batch_size=32,
    validation_split=0.2,
    callbacks=[
        tf.keras.callbacks.EarlyStopping(
            monitor='val_loss',
            patience=10,
            restore_best_weights=True
        )
    ],
    verbose=1
)
\end{lstlisting}

Early stopping monitors validation loss with patience 10, preventing overfitting while
ensuring that the best checkpoint is retained.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{result}[ConvGRU Baseline Performance]
Accuracy: \textbf{69.5\%} \quad F1-Score: 0.734
\end{result}

The ConvGRU underperforms the classical models on this dataset. This is consistent
with known behaviour: deep temporal models require substantially more labelled sequential
data to surpass ensemble methods on tabular features~\cite{suman2025}. The 3\,104-sample
training set is an order of magnitude smaller than what ConvGRU architectures typically
require for reliable temporal generalisation. The result nonetheless validates the
architecture's functional implementation and establishes a baseline for adversarial
evaluation.

\section{Adversarial Attack: Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Threat Model}

An adversary who understands the features monitored by an IDS can attempt to subvert
it by carefully controlling transmission behaviour to produce features that the model
misclassifies as benign. FGSM provides a systematic procedure: the attacker computes
the gradient of the model's loss with respect to the input features and nudges each
feature in the direction that maximises the loss~\cite{mansourian2025}:

\[
    \tilde{x} = x + \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}\!\left(\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(\theta, x, y)\right)
\]

In a vehicular context this could mean modulating transmission power to shift an RSSI
fingerprint, or throttling packet rate to keep PDR just above the detection threshold.

\subsection{Implementation for Random Forest}

Tree-based models do not have analytic gradients, so gradient approximation via
finite differences is used:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={FGSM implementation for Random Forest (gradient approximation).},
                   label=lst:fgsm_rf]
def fgsm_attack_rf(model, X, y, epsilon=0.1):
    """Finite-difference gradient approximation for tree ensembles."""
    X_adv = X.copy()
    h = 1e-4   # perturbation step for finite differences

    for i in range(X.shape[1]):
        X_plus  = X_adv.copy();  X_plus[:, i]  += h
        X_minus = X_adv.copy();  X_minus[:, i] -= h

        # Approximate gradient of cross-entropy loss
        probs_plus  = model.predict_proba(X_plus)[:, 1]
        probs_minus = model.predict_proba(X_minus)[:, 1]
        grad = (probs_plus - probs_minus) / (2 * h)

        # Multiply by label sign so gradient points toward wrong class
        grad_signed = np.sign(grad * (2 * y - 1))
        X_adv[:, i] += epsilon * grad_signed

    return X_adv

X_test_adv = fgsm_attack_rf(rf, X_test, y_test, epsilon=0.1)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Implementation for ConvGRU}

For the neural network, automatic differentiation provides exact gradients:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={FGSM implementation for ConvGRU using TensorFlow autodiff.},
                   label=lst:fgsm_convgru]
def fgsm_attack_nn(model, X, y, epsilon=0.1):
    X_tf = tf.Variable(X.astype(np.float32))
    y_tf = tf.constant(y.astype(np.float32).reshape(-1, 1))

    with tf.GradientTape() as tape:
        predictions = model(X_tf, training=False)
        loss = tf.keras.losses.binary_crossentropy(y_tf, predictions)

    gradient = tape.gradient(loss, X_tf)
    perturbation = epsilon * tf.sign(gradient)
    X_adv = X_tf + perturbation

    return X_adv.numpy()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Impact of FGSM}

\begin{result}[Model Performance Under FGSM ($\epsilon = 0.1$)]
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Clean Accuracy} & \textbf{Under FGSM} \\
\midrule
Random Forest & 91.7\% & \textbf{50.0\%} ($\downarrow$ 41.7pp) \\
ConvGRU       & 69.5\% & \textbf{63.6\%} ($\downarrow$ 5.9pp) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{result}

Random Forest's collapse to exactly 50\% accuracy (random-chance level) under FGSM
is particularly striking. It occurs because the finite-difference gradient effectively
identifies the decision boundaries of every tree in the ensemble, and a sufficiently
large $\epsilon$ pushes virtually every test sample across at least one boundary. The
ConvGRU shows smaller degradation in accuracy terms; however, its F1-score drops from
0.734 to 0.470, indicating severe precision collapse on the attack class.

\section{Adversarial Defence: Retraining}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Adversarial Retraining Strategy}

The primary defence evaluated is adversarial training: augmenting the training set with
adversarially perturbed samples generated from the training data, then retraining the
model on the combined clean + adversarial data~\cite{mansourian2025}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adversarial retraining for Random Forest.}, label=lst:adv_retrain_rf]
# Generate adversarial training examples
X_train_adv = fgsm_attack_rf(rf, X_train, y_train, epsilon=0.1)

# Combine clean and adversarial training data
X_train_combined = np.vstack([X_train, X_train_adv])
y_train_combined = np.hstack([y_train, y_train])

# Retrain on augmented dataset
rf_retrained = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=200,
    max_depth=15,
    random_state=42
)
rf_retrained.fit(X_train_combined, y_train_combined)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Adversarial retraining for ConvGRU.}, label=lst:adv_retrain_nn]
X_train_seq_adv = fgsm_attack_nn(model, X_train_seq,
                                  y_train_seq, epsilon=0.1)

X_combined = np.vstack([X_train_seq, X_train_seq_adv])
y_combined = np.hstack([y_train_seq, y_train_seq])

model.fit(X_combined, y_combined, epochs=30, batch_size=32,
          validation_split=0.2, verbose=0)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Recovery Results}

\begin{result}[Model Performance After Adversarial Retraining]
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Under FGSM} & \textbf{After Retraining} \\
\midrule
Random Forest & 50.0\% & \textbf{94.6\%} \\
ConvGRU       & 63.6\% & \textbf{69.0\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{result}

Adversarial retraining restores Random Forest to 94.6\%, which \textit{exceeds} its
original clean-data baseline of 91.7\% by 2.9 percentage points. The augmented training
data from adversarial perturbations effectively provides additional boundary exploration,
acting as implicit regularisation. ConvGRU recovers partially but remains limited by
the small dataset size and insufficient temporal context in the 4-window sequences.

\subsection{Key Findings}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]

\item \textbf{XGBoost achieves the best clean performance} (93.9\%), followed by
adversarially retrained Random Forest (94.6\%), then baseline Random Forest (91.7\%),

\item \textbf{FGSM is devastatingly effective against Random Forest:} accuracy drops
from 91.7\% to exactly 50.0\% (random chance). This confirms the adversarial
vulnerability gap identified as the central open problem in the literature survey.
The finite-difference gradient approximation correctly identifies decision boundaries
across the entire 200-tree ensemble.

\item \textbf{ConvGRU is more naturally robust to FGSM:} accuracy decreases by only
5.9pp, whereas the F1-score drops 26pp, indicating that the model is pushed toward
predicting the majority class rather than random assignment.

\item \textbf{Adversarial retraining is a highly effective defence:} Random Forest
recovers to 94.6\%, exceeding its original baseline. This demonstrates that the model
learns to classify in regions near adversarial examples, which simultaneously improves
generalisation.

\item \textbf{The heading--speed cosine similarity feature} emerged as a novel
contribution of the feature engineering design. It encodes the physical constraint that
a vehicle's reported direction of motion must be consistent with its reported velocity,
providing a cross-field consistency check analogous to the RSSI-position check in the
RSDM paper~\cite{li2019}.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature Importance Analysis}

Random Forest's feature importance scores (mean decrease in Gini impurity) confirm the
theoretical predictions from the literature:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \texttt{msg\_count} ranks as the highest-importance feature for DoS detection,
    consistent with the finding that channel flooding is most directly visible in packet
    rate.
    \item \texttt{pos\_noise\_mean} and \texttt{spd\_noise\_mean} are the next most
    important features, confirming that noise injection attacks (ConstPosOffset,
    RandomSpeedOffset) leave clear traces in the injected noise magnitudes.
    \item \texttt{hs\_cos\_mean} ranks in the top 5, validating the heading--speed
    consistency check as a meaningful detector of false data injection attacks.
    \item \texttt{spd\_mag\_max} is the primary discriminator for constant and random
    speed attacks, where attackers inject unrealistically high velocity values.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Validation of Literature Claims}

The experimental results empirically validate or nuance several specific claims from the
surveyed papers:

\textbf{Claim from~\cite{alkhulaidi2024}:} ``Random Forest is the most consistently
accurate and robust classifier on V2V and V2I traffic datasets, with PDR and RSSI as
the most discriminative variables.''

\textbf{Experimental validation:} Random Forest achieves 91.7\% accuracy, second only
to XGBoost. Feature importance analysis confirms that physical-layer-derived features
(noise magnitudes, speed consistency) are the most discriminative, consistent with the
claim.

\textbf{Claim from~\cite{mansourian2025}:} ``State-of-the-art VANET IDS models can
suffer severe accuracy degradation under small adversarial perturbations.''

\textbf{Experimental validation:} This is directly confirmed — RF drops to 50\% under
$\epsilon = 0.1$ FGSM perturbation. The literature predicted exactly this behaviour,
and the implementation reproduces it quantitatively.

\textbf{Claim from~\cite{suman2025}:} ``ConvGRU achieves competitive accuracy on VANET
benchmarks while maintaining inference times compatible with real-time embedded
deployment.''

\textbf{Experimental partial validation:} The ConvGRU achieves 69.5\% accuracy, which
is below the ensemble baselines. This divergence from the published result is expected:
the original ConvGRU paper trains on full CICIDS2017 and Car-Hacking datasets with
hundreds of thousands of samples. The 3\,104-sample subset used here is insufficient
for a deep sequential model to converge to its full potential.

\subsection{Design Limitations and Future Work}

\subsubsection{Dataset Subsetting}
The 3\,104-sample balanced subset is necessitated by the computational constraints of
the Kaggle T4 GPU environment. Processing all 22 million records would require
distributed computing infrastructure. The subset preserves attack-type stratification
but cannot represent the full temporal complexity of a multi-hour vehicular scenario.

\subsubsection{Wireless Metric Fidelity}
The VeReMi dataset does not contain explicit RSSI measurements. The noise fields
(\texttt{pos\_noise}, \texttt{spd\_noise}) serve as proxies but are generated by the
simulator's Gaussian noise model rather than a realistic propagation simulator. Future
work should evaluate on datasets generated with SUMO+OMNeT++ co-simulation including
explicit RSSI from the Friis or two-ray ground models.

\subsubsection{Adversarial $\epsilon$ Calibration}
The perturbation budget $\epsilon = 0.1$ is set as a fixed value in the experiments.
A more rigorous adversarial evaluation would sweep $\epsilon$ from 0 to 0.5 and plot
accuracy degradation curves, identifying the specific $\epsilon$ at which each model
fails catastrophically.

\subsubsection{Continual Learning}
Attack strategies evolve continuously. The current models require full retraining when
new attack types emerge. Incremental learning methods that update parameters without
catastrophic forgetting of previously learned patterns are an important future direction
for embedded OBU deployment~\cite{suman2025}.

\subsubsection{C-V2X Readiness}
All surveyed work and this implementation focus on IEEE\,802.11p/DSRC. The shift to
Cellular-V2X (C-V2X) in 5G NR-V2X changes the physical-layer metric landscape and
would require re-evaluation of the feature engineering assumptions, particularly RSSI
interpretation under beamforming.

\section{Conclusion}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This project has implemented, evaluated, and adversarially tested a complete intrusion
detection pipeline for VANETs on the VeReMi dataset. The following conclusions are drawn:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]

    \item \textbf{Time-window feature engineering} over 1-second intervals produces
    a compact 28-dimensional feature vector that captures the physical-layer signatures
    of 19 distinct VANET attack types. The heading--speed cosine similarity feature
    emerges as a novel physically-grounded consistency check.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble classifiers dominate:} XGBoost (93.9\%) and Random Forest
    (91.7\%) substantially outperform ConvGRU (69.5\%) on the available dataset.
    This confirms the known principle that tree ensembles outperform deep models on
    small tabular datasets, while deep models have the potential to surpass them at scale.

    \item \textbf{The adversarial vulnerability gap is real and severe:} FGSM reduces
    Random Forest accuracy from 91.7\% to 50.0\%, directly confirming the most
    significant open problem identified in the literature survey.

    \item \textbf{Adversarial retraining is effective:} Random Forest recovers to
    94.6\% after retraining on a combined clean + adversarial dataset, \textit{exceeding}
    its original baseline performance. This validates adversarial training as a viable
    and practical defence for embedded vehicular systems.

    \item \textbf{Three open problems} require future attention: (a) dataset realism
    with full physical-layer RSSI simulation; (b) adversarial robustness as a design
    constraint rather than an afterthought; and (c) continual learning for embedded
    OBUs that must adapt to evolving attack strategies without full retraining.

\end{enumerate}

The central argument of the literature survey is validated empirically: VANET attacks
exploit the open radio medium, and the most informative detection signals are the
physical-layer metrics that directly reflect what the channel is experiencing. The
proposed pipeline demonstrates that these signals, combined with rigorous adversarial
evaluation, provide a strong foundation for secure, lightweight VANET intrusion detection
in realistic deployments.
